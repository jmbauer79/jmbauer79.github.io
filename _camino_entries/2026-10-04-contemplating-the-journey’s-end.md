    It was still dark when I awoke, though I wouldn’t say I slept in. The sun
    doesn’t rise until past 8 this time of year in this part of Spain. Hadrien
    and Lizzie were asleep. The three of us had been placed upstairs in
    the addition outside the main entrance of the albergue. The others, Ulrika,
    Dominic, and Stef, were inside the main dormitory. I went downstairs to see
    if I might find some coffee.  


    The front door to the main albergue was locked. I remained outside in the courtyard for a few moments, breathing in the cool fresh air and watching a plump little sparrow singing on the arm of a chair.

content_block_2:
  narrative: >-
    I was hoping the front door would open any moment and release the aroma of a
    fresh roast. I texted Ulrika to see if she was awake and to ask if there was
    coffee inside. No response.  


    I decided to go back upstairs to my bunk. Hadrien and Lizzie still hadn’t awoken. They looked so peaceful, sleeping in the room together. I decided I would give them some space this morning, which, though they didn’t say so, I felt they wanted. I packed up as quietly as I could and whispered to them, “I’m heading out. I’ll see you on the trail.” Though the sun hadn’t risen, it felt like a late start, but it turned out I was the first of my group to leave that morning, so I walked alone.  


    It took me 40 minutes to reach a cafe where I was rewarded with the coffee I so craved, along with a Spanish tortilla and some fresh juice.  


    I was soon joined by Ulrika and Dominic who had started their day not long after I. The three of us enjoyed coffee and continued on together.  


    This wasn’t a “why-are-you-on-the-Camino" conversation day. We discussed equity and equality, opportunity and outcome, feminism and black lives matter. Dominic brought the perspective of a cis-gendered, straight, married, white man from Belgium. Ulrika, a single white female from Sweden. I held the role of the partnered gay man from California. The conversation was respectful but passionate. Dominic tended to take the opposing positions of those of Ulrika and me, or at least to make arguments or ask questions to pressure-test our arguments if not to offer a counterpoint.  


    During a break in the conversation, Dominic walked ahead. He was taking some space to process, Ulrika presumed. An hour of debate could exhaust anyone, especially if it felt like two against one.  


    Ulrika and I moved forward with lighter conversation and a focus on the Camino. We crossed the 12th century Ponte de San Xoán de Furelos, an arched stone bridge pilgrims have used to reach the other side of the Furelos River for over 800 years.  


    In town, we encountered some labrador puppies and their fecund parents followed by a tiny Jesus. A handsome donkey greeted us back on the trail.

  narrative: >-
    We caught up to Dominic and the three of us agreed to look for lunch.
    Dominic is vegan so options are limited. We found a cafe with few options,
    but Ulrika and Dominic were happy to sit and have a beverage and fruit. I
    decided I’d go on without them. It wasn’t goodbye. I just wanted some quiet
    time after all the talking earlier, and I knew I’d see them both later that
    day or the next. It was collectively understood on the Camino that one could
    decide to walk alone without needing to provide a reason. Neither Ulrika nor
    Dominic questioned my departure and bid me farewell.  


    While I was looking forward to walking alone, I continued to be open to the possibility of connecting with other pilgrims on the trail.  


    I did end up taking one break that afternoon. Though I planned to have a coffee, I saw a group of four Spanish pilgrims, two men and two women, having beers. I took their lead, ordered a beer at the bar, walked to their table, and asked (in Spanish) if I could sit with them.  


    The women were indifferent, “Como quieres.” The men welcomed me more warmly. 


    We discussed our respective reasons for walking the Camino. They were from Andalucia and explained they had only a week to walk, that it was difficult to get more time off of work. They observed that most pilgrims seemed to be foreigners.  


    “Does that bother you?” I asked. 


    “No. It’s a source of pride for me.” 


    They finished their beers and got back on the trail. I didn’t stay much longer. I wanted to reach Arzúa soon, the town where I would be staying that evening. I heard from Pol on WhatsApp. Originally, he was going to stay in Arzúa that evening as well but decided instead to walk further. He asked me when I’d be arriving because he wanted a chance to say goodbye before continuing. It would likely be our last chance to see each other during the trip. I arrived around 3 with Pol packed and ready to go. He had a long day ahead of him but was in great spirits.

    I took advantage of my early arrival to do some laundry and grab a glass of
    wine at the main plaza to catch up on journaling and enjoy some
    people-watching.  


    I saw a text come in from Hadrien an hour or so after sitting down. He and Lizzie had just arrived in Arzúa and asked what I was up to. I let them know where I was sitting in the plaza and invited them to join me for dinner and drinks. They were thrilled to accept. They just needed to shower and change. They’d be staying at the same albergue as I.  


    It hadn’t even been 12 hours since that morning, but the reunion brought bright smiles to each of our faces as they walked up. Any lingering doubt whether they wanted my company was lifted. We received our drinks, put in orders for some paella, and eagerly asked about each other’s days. Enhancing my recap with real faces, the Spanish pilgrims from earlier ended up at the same plaza and waved hello enthusiastically, as if right on cue.
